Demand Forecasting for Data Analysts 2026: Baselines, WAPE and When AI Models Actually Help

A forecast is only useful if it beats the simplest alternative. Before reaching for gradient boosting or a time-series foundation model, an analyst needs three things: a baseline to beat, an accuracy metric that fits the business, and a backtest that doesn't leak the future. Here is a practical workflow, with code you can run today.

Key takeaways
  • Always compare against a seasonal naive baseline (“same period last year”). If a model can't beat it in a backtest, don't ship it.
  • For retail data use WAPE together with bias, not MAPE: MAPE breaks on zero-sales periods and over-weights small items.
  • Backtest with a rolling, time-ordered split and a horizon equal to your decision lead time; never shuffle a time series.
  • AI foundation models (Chronos-2, TimesFM 2.5, Moirai 2.0, TiRex) are strong zero-shot candidates on periodic, human-driven series, but published benchmarks are mixed on noisy data — test them like any other model.
  • Use the XYZ classes from ABC-XYZ analysis to decide where precise forecasting is worth the effort.

Why Forecasting Is an Analyst Skill, Not Just a Data-Science Task

Every reorder point, staffing plan and promotion budget contains a forecast — written down or not. When a category manager asks “how many will we sell next month?”, the analyst who answers with a number, an error range and a clear method becomes the person the business plans around. Demand forecasting is the process of estimating future customer demand from historical data and known drivers such as seasonality, price and promotions.

It also closes the loop with the rest of category analytics. ABC-XYZ classification tells you which products matter and which are predictable; forecasting turns that into order quantities; assortment decisions then use the same demand patterns.

Start With Baselines (and Code to Run Them)

A baseline is a deliberately simple forecast that any real model must beat. It costs minutes to build and protects you from shipping a complicated model that is worse than “repeat last year”.

BaselineRuleHard to beat when…
NaiveNext value = last observed valueThe series is stable and has no seasonality
Moving averageMean of the last N periodsDemand is noisy but flat
Seasonal naiveSame period of the previous cycle (e.g. same week last year)Seasonality is strong — most retail categories
Seasonal averageAverage of the same period over the last 2–3 cyclesSeasonal and noisy at the same time

Here is a minimal, runnable comparison of a seasonal naive and a naive baseline, scored with WAPE and bias:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def wape(actual, forecast):
    """Weighted absolute percentage error: total error / total actual volume."""
    return np.abs(actual - forecast).sum() / np.abs(actual).sum()

def bias(actual, forecast):
    """> 0 means over-forecasting, < 0 means under-forecasting."""
    return (forecast - actual).sum() / actual.sum()

# y: weekly sales as a pandas Series with a DatetimeIndex
H, SEASON = 13, 52                      # forecast 13 weeks ahead, yearly cycle
train, test = y.iloc[:-H], y.iloc[-H:]

# seasonal naive: "the same 13 weeks last year"
snaive = pd.Series(train.iloc[-SEASON:-SEASON + H].values, index=test.index)
# plain naive: repeat the last observed value
naive = pd.Series(train.iloc[-1], index=test.index)

print(f"seasonal naive  WAPE={wape(test, snaive):.1%}  bias={bias(test, snaive):+.1%}")
print(f"naive           WAPE={wape(test, naive):.1%}  bias={bias(test, naive):+.1%}")

# Example output on a synthetic weekly series with a yearly cycle:
# seasonal naive  WAPE=5.9%  bias=-0.3%
# naive           WAPE=7.6%  bias=-7.2%
✅ Report the baseline's score next to every model's score. “WAPE 18%” means nothing alone; “WAPE 18% vs 24% for seasonal naive” is a decision.

Choosing an Accuracy Metric: WAPE, MAPE, Bias, MASE

The metric you pick decides which model “wins”, so it must reflect the business cost of being wrong.

MetricIdeaStrengthWatch out for
MAPEAverage of |error| ÷ actual per periodEasy to explainUndefined when actual = 0; heavily penalizes low-volume items
WAPETotal |error| ÷ total actual volumeVolume-weighted; works with zero days in aggregateCan hide large errors on small items
Bias(Total forecast − total actual) ÷ total actualReveals systematic over- or under-forecastingPositive and negative errors cancel out
MASEError scaled by the in-sample naive errorComparable across series with different scalesLess intuitive for business readers

In practice, report WAPE and bias together: WAPE says how far off you are, bias says in which direction. A forecast with low WAPE but a persistent positive bias quietly builds overstock.

Backtesting Without Fooling Yourself

Backtesting means pretending to be in the past, forecasting what happened next, and scoring it against reality. Done carelessly, it produces numbers that collapse in production.

  1. Set the horizon from the decision. If the supplier lead time is four weeks, a four-week-ahead forecast is the one that matters.
  2. Use a rolling origin. Train up to date t, forecast the next H periods, move t forward, repeat — and average the scores over many windows, not one lucky split.
  3. Score every model on identical windows, including the baselines.
  4. Prevent leakage. Use only information that would have been known at forecast time — no actual promo flags that were decided later, no future prices.
  5. Slice the results by ABC/XYZ segment and category, not just the grand total, where errors average out.
⚠️ Random (shuffled) cross-validation is wrong for time series: it lets the model train on the future and test on the past, which flatters every model.

Where Models Beat Baselines — and Where They Don't

Classical statistical models (ETS, ARIMA, Theta)

Strong defaults for individual series with trend and seasonality, fast to fit, and easy to explain. Always worth including in the comparison.

Gradient boosting with features

A good fit when you have many related series and real drivers — price, promotions, holidays, weekday — because one model can learn across products.

Time-series foundation models

Pre-trained models such as Chronos-2, TimesFM 2.5, Moirai 2.0 and TiRex produce zero-shot forecasts: no per-series training needed. Recent benchmark studies report competitive or better results on periodic, human-driven series such as traffic or electricity load, while on high-noise series such as financial returns, simple benchmarks and classical models can still win.

Treat a foundation model as one more candidate in the backtest, not as the default. If it beats your seasonal naive baseline and your classical models on your data, use it; if not, you have saved yourself a complicated dependency.

SituationReasonable starting point
One or a few strongly seasonal seriesSeasonal naive → ETS / Theta
Hundreds of products with price and promo driversGradient boosting with calendar and price features
Many series, little time to tuneFoundation model as a zero-shot candidate, scored against baselines
Mostly-zero, sporadic salesSee the next section — don't forecast each day

Intermittent Demand and the Long Tail

Many SKUs sell on a handful of days a month. Intermittent demand breaks ordinary models and metrics: day-level forecasts are mostly noise, and MAPE is undefined on the zero days. Three practical moves:

  • Aggregate to a level where signal appears — week instead of day, category instead of SKU.
  • Use methods built for sporadic demand, such as Croston's method and its variants.
  • Don't over-invest. For Z-class items (erratic demand in XYZ analysis) simple min/max rules and safety stock usually beat a precise forecast that cannot exist.

Forecast effort belongs where value and predictability meet: A-class items with X or Y demand. That is exactly what the ABC-XYZ matrix is for.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good WAPE for demand forecasting?

There is no universal threshold: achievable WAPE depends on volume, forecast horizon and product type. The useful question is whether your model beats the seasonal naive baseline on the same data and by how much.

Should I use MAPE or WAPE?

For retail and e-commerce data, prefer WAPE (plus bias). MAPE is undefined when actual sales are zero and over-weights low-volume items, while WAPE weights errors by sales volume.

Do I need deep learning to forecast demand?

No. Start with baselines and classical methods, then add gradient boosting or a foundation model only if a proper rolling backtest shows a clear improvement.

How much history do I need?

As a rule of thumb, at least two full seasonal cycles — about two years of data for yearly seasonality — so that a seasonal model can tell seasonality from noise.
